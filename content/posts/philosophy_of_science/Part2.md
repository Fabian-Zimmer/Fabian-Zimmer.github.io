This article is about part 2 of the anthology [Philosophy of Science: Central Issues (1998)](https://philpapers.org/rec/CURPOS). This part revolves around the seminal work [The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962)](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/61539.The_Structure_of_Scientific_Revolutions) by Thomas Kuhn, as it proposed radical views on how scientists choose between theories. The debate that followed over the next decades revealed many flaws and inconsistencies on his view of the history of science, and included good arguments about the sources of rationality and objectivity in science.

Therefore, this part begins with a "Kuhnian" view on scientific change, and how (according to this view) abrupt and revolutionary changes are its defining characteristics. This view is then discussed and criticised, alternative views that differ in character and intensity being proposed. At the end, discussions about objectivity in science are introduced, as well as the importance of social aspects in science that are highlighted with historic examples.

### The apparent Necessity of Scientific Revolutions

According to Kuhn's historical analysis, the cumulative acquisition of knowledge and the notion that theories build upon previous ones by encompassing them in a larger and "higher" theory proves to be an exception to the rule of scientific development. Rather, a successor theory or paradigm required the dissolution and destruction of a competing or older one and its associated concepts. 

Some historic cases supposedly support this idea. One such example concerns the theory of heat. Before Newton's theory, heat in physical systems was attributed to a substance called the "caloric" that moves across the system from hotter to colder regions. Only with the complete dissolution of these ideas and concepts could we move to the paradigm, where heat was seen equivalent to (mechanical) motion of the constituents of the physical system, i.e. to the theory of thermodynamics. The idea of an additional substance for heat was incommensurable with the idea that heat is, in a way, an emergent quantity.

Another interesting example concerns the idea that Newton's theory cannot actually be derived from Einstein's theory. Although we can see Newton's theory as a set of statements that is a subset of statements of Einstein's theory, and that apply in the limit $v \ll c$, the physical referents of these Einsteinian concepts are by no means the same as the Newtonian concepts with the same name. Take the concept of mass as an example. Newtonian mass is conserved while Einsteinian is convertible with energy. Only at low speeds can they be measured in similar ways, but still shouldn’t be conceived the same way, as they entail completely different interpretations — one is static, one is transient. Hence, to go from one theory to another, we have to change the fundamental structural elements and their interpretations. Although the mathematical statements of Newton’s theory can be recovered by limiting the statements of Einstein’s theory and changing the meaning of their content, Einstein’s theory can be argued to require the destruction of the ideas of the previous theory to be a successor at all. 

As to why revolutionary changes have to take place, Kuhn appeals to the fact that only stubborn anomalies, i.e. those that cannot be reconciled within a current paradigm, lead to new discoveries. The only way forward then, is a radical change to a new paradigm, where these anomalies cease to be stubborn and become explainable.

### Kuhn's Views on Theory Choice

We encountered the demarcation question in part 1, that asks where the line between science and pseudoscience lies. A tempting avenue to explore is to enumerate all the values that should guide our debates regarding theory choice. Regarding values and the guiding principles that are supposed to be encapsulated, Kuhn discusses the following five:
- Accuracy: deductions and predictions based on theory should be in agreement with experiments and observations, as well as be able to explain them.
- Consistency: a theory should be free of logical contradictions (internal consistency), and harbour no contradictions with other accepted theories (external consistency).
- Scope: theoretical consequences should extend beyond the set of questions, observations, and laws the theory was initially designed to explain.
- Simplicity: a theory should unify isolated phenomena into a larger integrated whole.
- Fruitfulness: by disclosing new phenomena, a theory should encourage new research.

One of the views around his time was, that these criteria provide a shared basis for theory choice between an established paradigm and an upstart competitor. Kuhn argues, that no matter which values you employ, their interpretations vary across individual scientists (even practitioners of the same paradigm) and are too imprecise. They can also conflict with one another, perhaps accuracy favouring the well established theory but scope supporting the visionary competitor. As long as there exists no algorithm to dictate rational, unanimous theory choice — most philosophers belief such an algorithm as unattainable — all relies on subjective factors, idiosyncrasies, and ultimately extrarational choices. 

As an example, fruitfulness encapsulates a measure of how promising a theory appears to be in solving puzzles in the future. As this can not be known a priori, Kuhn argues this to require an element of faith that eludes rational evaluation. This was one of Kuhn's conclusions: subjectivity and touches of irrationality seem to have been the deciding factors in the history of scientific paradigm changes. As the Zeitgeist of the philosophical community involved rational humans doing objective science, his conclusions were received with trepidation.

Kuhn used a few avenues to argue for his views. One is the so-called "Theory-Ladennes of Observation": What scientists observe depends on the theories they accept, ergo epistemic components provided by observations and experiments alone can't play the decisive role in theory choice. A simple example to see what is meant by this, and which shows the implausibility of this thesis involves Kepler and Ptolemy watching the sun rise. Kepler sees the sun as stationary, whereas for Ptolemy the sun is a moving planet, so it apparently can’t be the same object they’re observing. But this is a fallacy of equivocation, where the object observed is conflated with the belief that observers have about that object. Another way of seeing it, is that if Ptolemy would be converted to Copernicanism, then he would have the magical power of changing components of the outside world by changing his beliefs.

Another argument is sometimes referred to as "Meaning Variance": As adherents of different paradigms mean different things when using the same words and phrases, any logical comparison is precluded and paradigms are incommensurable. We experience this in everyday life when we miscommunicate or talk past each other, simply because we hold different connotations to the same words. 
Meaning related arguments are hard to evaluate, mostly because we have no consensus on what meaning is in the first place. However, what makes Kuhn's incommensurability claim a non sequitur is that there are more layers to meaning than the one associated to a particular theory and its implicit definitions. For example, a layer of meaning in between that of objects and subjective ideas and conceptualisations about those objects was analysed in [On Sense and Reference by Gottlob Frege (1892)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2181485). The point here being, that some layers may serve as a common ground on which shared meaning can be found across individuals, across paradigms.

### Breaking a Paradigm into Pieces

Kuhn's arguments and views have been dissected by many philosophers. Larry Laudan has a particularly comprehensive critique regarding the historical accuracy of Kuhn's claims, which he laid out in [Dissecting the Holist Picture of Scientific Change (1984)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.1525/j.ctt1pnkj2) (chapter 4 of the linked work). The argumentation convincingly shows that the idea for paradigms to be seen as holistic entities, which one either fully accepts or rejects as a whole, is historically inaccurate and logically unsound.

The concept of a paradigm was used a few times already during this and my [previous article](https://fabian-zimmer.github.io/posts/philosophy_of_science/part1/) about part 1, and it is timely to see the several ways it can be defined.
1. A paradigm offers a conceptual framework of entities comprising the world and their interactions by making different claims about the nature of those entities and their modes of interaction.
2. A paradigm specifies methods used to inquire about those entities and interactions. 
3. A paradigm entails a set of goals, values, and ideals, which its proponents espouse.

The claim of Kuhn is that paradigm change requires a simultaneous (rather than sequential or piecemeal) change in all these three categories. He insisted that rationality must be relativised to choices within a paradigm rather than between paradigms, but nothing is offered to make rational choices during interparadigmatic debate still possible. The analogies "conversion experience" and "Gestalt-shift" were used by him for paradigm shifts, which spawned interpretations that invoked religious or irrational aspects. Larry Laudan now shows the view of paradigms as an inextricable, unchangeable mixture of ingredients (such as methods, values, goals, etc.) to be erroneous, because it misses an important part of the dynamics of paradigm shifts: changes within paradigms.

### Laudan loosens the fit

The three definitions above basically describe "levels" that are important for a paradigm: the theoretical, the methodological, and the axiological level. An often used premise in these philosophical discussions, was that these levels form a hierarchy: values and goals assume the top position, influencing the theories and prescribing the employed methods. Ernan McMullin in his [Rationality and Paradigm Change in Science (1993)](https://philpapers.org/rec/MCMRAP-2) describes certain "lower" values and methods to serve as means to ends, i.e. to attain or satisfy "higher" values and goals of science. Those higher ones are usually set (but debatable on moral grounds), whereas the lower ones justified in an instrumental fashion, their performance evaluated in terms of the goals. If discourse ensues on one level, one uses the concepts of one higher level to argue for a resolution. Naturally, there is a problem if there is disagreement as what permanent attributes of science should comprise the top layer. This is when "conversion experiences" happen.

Laudan now proposes the following. Instead of arranging the components of the three levels in a hierarchy, we reticulate them and suppose that each piece of the net is then negotiable in and of itself. Looking more closely at scientific history, it turns out that this model has high fidelity. The extensive scholarship since Kuhn's prominent work has shown, that we have collapsed and compressed the indeed gradual changes during scientific revolutions due to our temporal distance, which led to the illusion of abrupt and monumental shifts that happened in a short period. History is actually full of gradual, piecemeal replacements in the reticulational picture. Laudan lists two illustrating examples.

1. From the time of Francis Bacon (born in the 1560s) until the early 19th century, most scientists subscribed to the rules of inductive inference associated with Bacon, Hume, and Newton, precluding the postulation of any theoretical or hypothetical entities. Newton's famous phrase "hypotheses non fingo", that is "to feign (invent) no hypotheses" captures this attitude well. This methodological orientation largely vanished between circa 1800 and 1860, when it was replaced by the logic known as hypothetico-deduction: conjecture a hypothesis and logically deduce what associated observations or empirical work should then be possible. This shift in methods cannot be tied to any shifts in ontology or scientific values of that period.
2. It is well evidenced that, during the 19th century, certainty as the goal of science gave way to producing theories that were plausible, probable, or well tested. From the time of Aristotle onwards, scientists sought theories that were demonstrative and apodictically certain. By the end of the 19th century, most admitted that science could, at most, aspire to highly probable knowledge. This profound epistemic revolution was not specifically associated with the emergence of a new paradigm. On the contrary, fallibilism came to be associated with virtually every major research program, also for the paradigms at odds with each other: atomism and anti-atomism, wave theory and particle theory, Darwinism and Lamarckism, and so on. The axiological shift, independently of the theory and methods employed, was gradually inserted into all paradigms.

Contrary to what the holistic picture proclaims, shifts in each level could occur in a largely causally unconnected manner, although they are unquestionable interrelated. Each temporarily fixed point, representing a common ground between paradigms, could therefore be used to hold rational discussions on how to adjust the other nodes in the reticulated scientific enterprise. However, and this has been (and probably still is) debated, even those levels of agreement may sometimes be insufficient to terminate the controversy, which is part of Kuhn's "subjectivist" thesis. Nevertheless, Laudan soundly argues for the majority of scientific paradigm shifts to have occurred in this piecemeal fashion, and not as a forced "Gestalt-shift" on all levels or in the minds of the scientists.

### Fallacies in underdeterminism

A further contribution of Laudan is his critique of the four remaining arguments (the unsoundness of Kuhn's "Theory-Ladennes of Observation" and "Meaning Variance" arguments were shown above), which Kuhn relied on to make his case for the impotency of methodological rules and shared cognitive values to resolve large-scale scientific disagreement. They support his ideas related to "underdeterminism", a term which captures when certain construals are insufficient to resolve a certain situation in a clear way.

**The "Ambiguity of Shared Standards" argument.**
Kuhn believed that every shared value and principle can be interpreted, judged, and applied differently by different individuals, thus being too ambiguous and imprecise to resolve issues regarding theory choice. The important realisation is that, while indeed some values can indeed be ambiguous at times (like simplicity), many are not. Agreed on their nature by the scientific community, they have been used unambiguously throughout history. For example, consistency of an internal nature, i.e. a non-contradictory structure of a theory, is a matter of logic, proofs, and demonstrations, that are wholly independent of the individuals. Another straightforward example would be novel predictions, which is simply a matter of timely documentation and subsequent juxtaposition of predicted against observed reality. To generalise the ambiguity of one value to all is simply unwarranted.

**The “Collective Inconsistency of Rules” argument.**
A similar unjustified extrapolation is done for contradictions arising within a set of shared standards, for example empirical accuracy and generality of a theory. Again, Kuhn argues such disagreements to apply globally to all sets. Laudan identified this erroneous position, and shows that one can find plenty of consistent sets of principles documented and used throughout history, naming "Mill's methods" first described in [A System of Logic by John Stuart Mill (1843)](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139149839) as an example. Many historical examples provide evidence against such extrapolations, and using them to establish a general form for underdetermination becomes illegitimate.

**The “Shifting Standards” argument.**
Kuhn's notion that the holistic paradigms are incommensurable implies that shifts in the standards that govern and define the relevant problems and explanations necessarily accompany paradigm shifts. This argument is addressed by Laudan's reticulational model of scientific changes, which is far richer and explains historical examples better: disagreements about standards and their preferred methods simply do not have to, and indeed did not, coincide with ontological debates. Laudan finally reiterates the point, that one carries a misunderstanding when claiming that resolutions of disagreements cannot be anything but arbitrary closures, powered by rhetoric rather than rational argument. His reticulational model provides precisely a mechanism by which scientists can identify other shared standpoints even if, say, their standards disagree. Disagreements at the axiological level thus do not demand anything irrational about their resolutions.

**The “Problem-Weighting” argument.**
At times, scientists assign different "weights" to different (types of) problems and their (types of) allowed solutions. Thereby, issues that are seen as the most pressing and salient differ, and scientists adhere to the view that only their paradigm can address these issues and provide solutions. Debate about epistemic support of rival paradigms is therefore, Kuhn insists, inevitably inconclusive or that it must be resolved by factors outside rational scientific inquiry. However, the counterargument lies within this argument itself, in the form of a hidden premise: to rationally assign a degree, a "weight" of probative significance to a problem rests on one’s being able to show that there are viable methodological and epistemic grounds that enable such assignments. Exposing these chains of thought and reasoning allows scientists to reflect rationally on their choices, and the same mechanisms used for assigning their degrees in the first place can be used to solve interparadigmatic debate about epistemic significance of problems.

There is another topic of relevance to understand paradigms, which relates to the "Problem-Weighting" argument. Paradigms and their theories have sometimes been devised with a certain "central" problem in mind that needed resolution. However, the centrality of a problem on the paradigms’ explanatory agenda does not necessitate the assignment of a high degree of epistemic or probative weight to that problem when it comes to determining how well supported a certain theory or paradigm is. Historical examples, such as the revolution from "Phlogiston" theory to Dalton's chemistry entailed a "loss" of explained problems according to the old theory, which did not however make the new paradigm less supported. On the contrary, instances of greatest probative weight in the history of science have not been high on the list of problems that theories were developed to solve. They are precisely the instances that test the theory most "severely", to use Karl Popper's words. Some historical examples listed by Laudan, which were generally not high on the list of problems that scientists developed their theories to solve, were the bending of light near the Sun or the recession of Mercury’s perihelion for Einstein's theory, the Arago disk experiment around Faraday's times for theories of magnetism, or the reconstitution of white light from the spectrum around Newton's times for optic theories. What this has to do with paradigm changes, is that Kuhn (and others) have confused the interests of scientists to solve certain problems (the central ones, which motivated the construction of their theories) with the scientist's judgement concerning how well a paradigm is evidentially supported (via the problems of great probative weight). Conflation of the two can entail the erroneous conclusion, that scientists automatically differ in their epistemic judgements of which theories are well tested, and that irrational means are necessary to resolve their differences.

### Objectivity in science

It is commonly believed that science is objective due its impartial and neutral view on nature. This "view from nowhere", as for example the philosopher Thomas Nagel put it, is supposedly achieved by relying on non-subjective, non-arbitrary criteria for developing, accepting, and rejecting hypotheses and theories that constitute the view. The popular reverence for science is largely based on this belief, but also on the idea that the empirical data used is intrinsically objective. The latter, of course, presupposes that it is accumulated in a robust and honest way, and systematic errors of experiments are understood or non-existent.

Building upon this, the positivist movement within philosophy of science recognised only that which can be scientifically verified or logically (mathematically) proven, rejecting metaphysics and theism, and claiming to be as objective as possible. When asked on how to account for the role of the inevitable subjective aspect in scientific inquiry, the positivists resort to the following contextual distinction.

**Context of Discovery:**
This context takes care of the subjective influences on the initial formulation of a hypothesis (dreams, intuitive guesses, or other aspects of the mental or emotional life of scientists) but views them strictly as features of an individual's psychology, standing apart from the scientific process.

**Context of Discovery:**
In this context, the generative factors associated with the context of discovery are disregarded. The hypothesis is now only considered with respect to its observable consequences that determine its acceptability, purged of any subjective factors, thus rendering scientific inquiries objective.

Adherents of this contextual view isolate subjective influences, effectively treating them as inconsequential quirks of humans and their psyches, and appeal to the objectivity that is intrinsic to the scientific method. To shorten a lengthy discussion and summarise previously encountered arguments, Kuhn points out that this still does not make these intellectual activities objective, because they are carried out in the context of a paradigm. Helen Longino in her work [Science as Social Knowledge (1990)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvx5wbfz) investigates the nature and emergence of objectivity in science more carefully, and argues both Kuhn's view and the contextualist approach to be subject to the same problems. Her key contribution is a shift in perspective: instead of viewing the scientific enterprise as an aggregate of solitary contributions of isolated genius, she enlarges the view until the social web that underlies science becomes visible.

### Objectivity by degrees

The history of scientific development may at a glance be perceived as the result of solitary acts of genius. The successful theories appear to have been developed in isolation by a few individuals and their intellectual prowess. Only on closer inspection does survivorship bias reveal itself, and only when accounting for the social structure all these individuals were inevitably embedded in does the emergence of objectivity become clearer. Longino shows how the social aspects of science manifest, to a large degree, in three ways:
1. Individual scientists are dependent on one another for their practice, as otherwise their ideas cannot proliferate or their sophisticated instruments and experiments cannot be built.
2. Initiation into scientific inquiry requires education, learning traditions, and techniques from individuals having undergone similar initiation and already "living" in the scientific world to be entered.
3. Scientists represent networks embedded in a society, and therefore depend for their survival on that society's views, specifically whether their work is valued and supported.

Social interactions underlie all three. Longino argues objectivity to be, in a way, an emergent property that comes from criticism and peer-review of scientific content. Ideas for theories and experiments are refined by the myriad of minds that comprise the community, and what knowledge may proliferate is essentially decided by these social processes. Most importantly, the questioning of the relevance and function of evidence, as asked by a diverse pool of personalities, can expose the background beliefs, assumptions, and biases that are present within that which is questioned — the theories, experiments, and psyches of individual scientists.

The word dialogue is composed of the Greek words of *dia*, meaning "through" or "across", and *logos*, perhaps translatable to "speech" or "reason". Monologue can be considered the opposite pole of a continuum. Objectivity, then, arises as the scientific enterprise moves along this continuum from monologue to dialogue. A single perspective of a scientist is transformed into a multifaceted amalgam of views. The intersubjective criticism of the community transforms an initially telescopic view on reality into a kaleidoscopic one. Objectivity of scientific inquiry is thus a consequence of it's being a social, not an individual, enterprise. There simply exists no objective method that each individual can follow.

To sustain this transformative critical discourse, some criteria have to be met. The existence of platforms for presenting criticism such as journals or conferences is necessary. A list of values can guide scientific criticism. Although they are subject to the weighting problem or the problem of ambiguity as discussed above, it is precisely this open-ended and non-consistent nature of these standards that allows for pluralism and diversity in the sciences. We must realise that such disagreements as seen throughout the history of science are vital to its survival. Lastly, Longino emphasises that sexual and racial asymmetries must also be remedied, as it will increase the diversity of viewpoints, which further increases the degree of objectivity in scientific communities for the reasons outlined above.

### Acknowledging contextual values

A central theme of this article so far has been the changing views on shared values and how they dictate theory choice. The discussions above were about the *cognitive values* (sometimes also referred to as epistemic values) that concern cognition and knowledge. Naively, we would expect the scientific enterprise to place the value of truth at the top, but in reality most truths have little scientific value (counting hairs on dogs is one example). Although the values discussed above, such as generality or predictive power, are related to truth, they are not reducible to truth in any obvious way. Furthermore, theories cannot be known a priori to be true, so experiments must be performed which then rely on other methods and values themselves. So even if truth were the primary virtue of science, value debates are inevitable, and as argued above, this is a good thing: they help adapt the nature of science and distinguish it from other truth-seeking enterprises such as genealogy, logic, or theology.

Contextual values, on the other hand, are things such as norms, preferences, beliefs, and interests unrelated to the cognitive aims of science. They can be ideological, leading to Nazi Germany rejecting Einstein's theories because he was Jewish, or take the form of sexual or racial biases. These values depend more strongly on society and culture, vary over time, and can be transformed or rejected without practitioners ceasing to do science. 

Some attempts were made to rid the epistemic processes of such contextual values, in a quest to discover the ultimate objective methods for science. One example discussed above was the positivists differentiating between the context of discovery (which they later neglected) and the context of justification (which they appealed to for objectivity). The *value-neutrality* thesis refers to such a view, where science is free from contextual values, and if there were episodes of "tainted" science in history they were rare. Apart from Helen Longino rejecting this to be an unachievable ideal, Kathleen Okruhlik in her work [Gender and the Biological Sciences (1994)](https://doi.org/10.1080/00455091.1994.10717393) makes a simple but strong argument as to why contextual factors inevitably affect the content of science and cannot be exterminated.

The key realisation for her argument lies within a simple characteristic of conjecturing and theorising: when we observe the world and come up with explanations, we compare the resulting hypotheses, in fact, not directly to nature in and of itself, but rather to other rival hypotheses. Therefore, even if we grant the decision procedure between hypotheses to be perfectly objective, if all the hypotheses have been generated, say, in a deeply sexist culture, then all scientific development will be contaminated by sexism, as non-sexist rival hypotheses will never even be generated. The idea that the social influences on scientific development only happen in the context of discovery and get purified in the context of justification does not make sense. And thus the claim that the content of science can be isolated against social influences is erroneous. 

Okruhlik shows a few historical examples, where the questions asked and hypotheses generated in the first place were deeply influenced by the biases and asymmetries of the scientific community under consideration. In the biological sciences for example, the initial microscopy perspective on the egg and sperm interactions was completely skewed from the outset due to an androcentric view, which expected the male part to "heroically fight" its way to the passive egg, making the scientists blind to the active role of the female counterpart. As another example, Darwin attributed evolutionary development exclusively to male activity. Anthropological evidence of tool usage and development was interpreted with the "man-the-hunter" story. However, explaining archaeological trends with a gynocentric perspective instead revealed insights into nutritional stress during pregnancy, how mothers fed their young, and how that in turn shaped tool development. 

### What we learned

There was an idea that the scientific method owes its success to it's being based on some kind of fundamental laws of discovery, which are intrinsically free from any kind of subjectivity. When practicing these methods one is able to adopt the ultimate "view from nowhere", allowing to make decisions objectively. In the course of scientific development, philosophers showed this to be an inconceivable quest, devoid of humanity. The arguments presented in this article show but only a glimpse of the debates that ensued, and the wisdom that has been cultivated due to pushing and pulling against such ideas. 

The pendulum swung quite far to the opposite side, when Kuhn presented his ideas of the irrationality of scientists to be deciding factors. The society of his time also experienced a descent into postmodernism, where all pretense of objectivity was abandoned and science was portrayed as just another story of the infinite prism of perspectives, any being as good as any other. 

Neither of these extremes has fully taken hold of the scientific community at large, however, and works that came later revealed new possibilities of how rationality and objectivity can be reintroduced. One thing so far is clear: humanity and its science form a dynamic relationship, where no participant completely dominates the other. Science is neither irrational, nor can scientific practitioners blindly follow some objective method. It is due to the effort of all of us, and the patiently and compassionately held discussions, that science can progress in a wholesome manner.
