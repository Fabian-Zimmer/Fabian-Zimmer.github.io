This article is about part one of the book [The Master and His Emissary (2009)](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/6968772-the-master-and-his-emissary) by Iain McGilchrist. In part one, Iain gives us a comprehensive overview of the anatomy of our brain, what cognitive functions and abilities are associated with which hemisphere, and how the hemispheres each see and interact with the self and the world, and how they provide the foundations for our subjective experience. Due to their frequent use, "right hemisphere" is abbreviated as RH and "left hemisphere" as LH.

The reader is of course advised to read the book itself, but due to its perhaps intimidating volume and language, it is understandable that this may not be easily done. Therefore, the aim of this article is to provide the reader with an "on-ramp" to the topic, by highlighting and summarising the essential elements that characterise the contents and thesis of the book, while still providing enough context and depth. 

## Causes for Lateralisation

Brain lateralisation is a widespread phenomenon in the animal kingdom. The left/right asymmetry in the central nervous system is especially strong across all vertebrate classes (amphibians, birds, fish, mammals, and reptiles). Other animal groups (e.g. arthropods or mollusks) show species-specific lateralisation (e.g. bees and ants, or octopuses). Motor control in animals with brain lateralisation is contralateral, i.e. the LH controls the right side of the body, and the RH the left side. It is also not a recent evolutionary trait, as bi-hemispheric structure goes back a long way down the phylogenic tree.

This seems to suggest, that there was a universal problem that biological organisms faced, and evolution brought forth an efficient solution that spread through almost all ecological niches. What common problem do all living things share, that are pressured by selective forces? The answer seems to lie with attention. For successful proliferation, an organism must have the capacity to do two things simultaneously: 
1. having a **broad and open awareness** of the environment and terrain, especially scanning for threats, like predators or natural hazards;
2. having a **narrow and focused attention** to pick out important things from this background environment, like food or potential mates.

These two modes require two very different kinds of attention. They reflect the conflict between individual needs, being in competition with others, and seeing oneself in context and part of a larger whole, in relation to others. 

Another motivation why a division of the brain seems beneficial is based on an observation by Charles Sherrington in 1906: human sensorimotor control relies on "opponent processors", i.e. dual forces that oppose and inhibit each other, allowing us to perform delicate procedures with our appendages. This argument could, however, be considered close to begging the question. 

Whatever the exact set of causes and conditions were that led to divided brains, animals who are lateralised show better capacity and ability in important survivalistic areas, like hunting, foraging, and socializing. 

## Anatomical Overview

Our divided brain is connected by the Corpus Callosum, a tissue containing millions of fibres that connect topologically similar regions of each hemisphere. Out of the $\mathcal{O}(80)$ billion neurons, on the $\mathcal{O}(20)$ billion are cortical neurons. But only about 2% of those cortical neurons are connected via this tissue, and such connections are often inhibitory, suppressing the communication across hemispheres. Remarkably, the Corpus Callosum has decreased in size with increasing overall brain size, becoming smaller during our evolutionary history. While the functional asymmetry between hemispheres grew, the interconnectedness lessened. We can tentatively conclude, that each hemisphere was increasingly less able to interfere with the other.

There are many differences between the hemispheres, both of an anatomical and a functional nature. To get a first impression of each hemisphere, the following columns capture some important aspects of each. All mentioned items and concepts will be frequently revisited, each time deepening our understanding.


{{< split 6 6>}}
### Right Hemisphere

The RH is longer, wider, and heavier. It has more and larger neurons, more dendritic branching, and more white matter, all arranged in a way that facilitates global communication across the whole hemisphere. The RH is more reliant on nor-adrenaline, which enables sustained performance over longer periods. Together with the facts that the RH is able to take in multiple types of sensory data together while having greater working memory, it is reasonably analogous to a parallel processor. All of this together provides a global, broad, and flexible attention that allows the RH to **present** the world — it sees things as a whole and embedded in context, which you are an integral part of. 

The RH presents the world as it is, and as a general rule, no models or predictions are involved. It is the part of our brain that can provide novelty to us. This is supported by studies showing metabolic activity in the RH to be increased when practicing new skills, or when new information enters peripheral vision. 

And finally, the frontal lobe of the RH is asymmetrically larger than the same region in the LH. Metabolism in this part is linked to activities concerning empathy, socialising, and in general the relationships between ourselves and individuals.

---
### Left Hemisphere

The LH shows many contrasting and opposite characteristics compared to the RH. Importantly, it is wired in a local fashion: regions within the LH are interconnected within themselves, with only relatively sparse connections across regions and larger distances. The resulting characteristic of the LH is its narrow-minded and self-referential nature. 

The LH hosts the language center, which is associated with its enlarged posterior region. Instead of nor-adrenaline, it relies more on dopamine. These two properties already characterise the LH well, and lay the foundations to the process most associated with the LH: logic and language. For linguistic phrases to be constructed, a chain of logical events that are ordered in a mostly serial and linear fashion is required. The motivation to complete these chains is provided by the euphoric sensations delivered by the dopamine micro doses. The last sentence is not meant as an incentive for the reader to picture the language center as a chemically motivated homunculus, but is simply a metaphor for the underlying neurological processes. Overall, the LH is reasonably analogous to a serial processor.

The LH receives what that which the RH presents. It then **re-presents** the world in de-contextualised abstractions, the whole broken into individual parts to be logically analysed with local and focused attention. Once category systems for stimuli and past events, as well as predictive models for the future have been built, the LH has changed novelty to familiarity, surprise to routine, presentation to re-presentation.

{{< /split >}}


The story above applies to the "standard" configuration of the brain shared by about 90% of people: the language center sits in the LH and the right-hand is dominant. The other 10% can have partial of complete hemispheric inversion, where some or all processes and centers usually associated with the LH are associated with the RH, and vice versa. Not much may happen in the latter (complete inversion) case, but unique benefits or deficits are documented for the former (partial inversion) case.

Generally, increased size comes with increased functional capacity. According to the overview above, the RH is overall more capable then. It will become clear later, that the RH is not just more powerful, but also has primacy.

## How is Evidence Gathered?

The study of the brain, from a cellular and neurological level up to psychological and social levels is an enormous field of study that goes back many decades. Various methods to study brain lateralisation have been developed over time, ranging from the primitive and invasive to non-invasive methods supported by modern technology. 

As soon as surgeons had the capacity to open the skull and surgically alter the brain directly, treatments and procedures such as the lobotomy developed that involved cutting connections to and from the prefrontal cortex. Even the removal of entire sections of the brain was made possible. The surviving subjects and the studies of their behaviour and cognitive capacities provided waves of data to study hemispheric differences.

An early treatment for epileptic seizures was to cut the connective tissues in and of the Corpus Callosum, leaving the two hemispheres partially incommunicado. The resulting so-called "split-brain" patients gave the lateralisation scientists unique insights into the inner life of the hemispheres when they are less impeded by the other, how they individually think, and how they see the world. In a similar manner, scientists could also learn a great deal from stroke victims, where certain brain regions of the subjects either ceased to function properly temporarily, or where parts of, or an entire hemisphere was permanently damaged. 

And finally, modern technological advancements allowed to study the brain in a non-invasive manner. Both the epidermal placement of electrodes to measure the electromagnetic activity in the brain, making an electroencephalogram (EEG), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques allowed to correlate metabolic activity in different areas of the brain with certain behaviours and cognitive activities. 

All of these methods, with their accumulated data over decades, led to the conclusions and resulting worldview as described in the book The Master and His Emissary. Phrasings like "the RH has..." or "the LH only cares about..." as used in the remainder of the article are not to be understood literally, but rather as metaphors for the findings of brain lateralisation studies. Of course there are a variety of difficulties in such studies or the associated statistical analyses that were performed. However, acknowledging the decades of working experience and the resulting nuanced understanding the author has himself in and of the field, as well as the vast amount of references and literature cited provide a solid evidential framework for the emerging picture, namely the way the hemispheres see and interact with the world.

## Philosophical Interlude

There are a few musings of a philosophical nature we can make at this point, before moving on to more tangible matters.

The most fascinating unknown related to the brain is that of mind, or consciousness. There is nothing remotely like it for means of comparison. It is phenomenologically and ontologically unique. That makes investigations difficult, since "All attempts at explanation depend, whether explicitly or implicitly, on drawing parallels between the thing to be explained and some other thing that we believe we already understand better.", as Iain puts it. The closest analogy he gives in a footnote is based on the metaphor of wave and water. Does the water movement cause the wave? Not really, for the flow of water **is** the wave. Analogously, the changing states of the brain do not cause the mind, but **are** the mind. But that’s where the analogy ends, since waves do not have this "inwardness" that the mind does through its self-reflection abilities.

Another matter encountered when studying the brain and mind, is that everything we know about them is itself a product of consciousness. The difficulty in the quest of determining whether mind depends on matter and/or is a product of the brain, is that everything we know about anything is itself a mental creation. What Iain emphasises at this point, is that the mind is more a process than a thing, a way of being rather than an entity. A process of interaction with whatever it is that exists apart from itself according to its own private history.

Finally, and of utmost importance for the central thesis of the book, is the following principle. Ontologically speaking, **attention is prior to functions and things**, since the world (in which functions and things exist) is altered depending on the way we attend it. Attention changes what kind of things come into being for us. In other words, while "cognitive functions" may be neutral, the way they are used via attention is how values enter, and how our perception of reality is being shaped. For example, two participants of a social interaction would have very different feelings and views when they treat each other as friends or as enemies, even though nothing objectively has changed. Science purports to not do this, claiming to instead objectively uncover the truth in everything with a "view from nowhere". However this stance is also value-laden in itself: valuing detachment of the observer to the observed thing. Useful, but not truer or more real than other viewpoints. 

## How the Hemispheres Contribute

Although the majority of brain hemisphere studies aim to show the differences between them, it is vital to understand that both hemispheres are involved in, and contribute in some way, to virtually every human activity. Apart from their clear anatomical differences as summarized in the two columns above, they fundamentally differ in the way in which they engage with and process experiences and information. There are a few examples that highlight the basics particularly well.

When it comes to problem solving, only the RH can delicately present an array of solutions that remain credible and alive while alternatives are explored. The LH simply takes a single solution that it already "knows" (e.g. one it has judged to work in the past) and tends to stick with it. A related process, creativity, is facilitated better by the ability of the RH to make looser and more distant conceptual connections and associations. Conversely, the LH can only build local, self-referential networks of thoughts, and furthermore suppresses this more relaxed thinking by inhibiting (via the connections in the Corpus Callosum) the RH in certain situations. 

Although the region of the brain that, metabolically speaking, is most active during linguistic task sits in the LH, the RH contributes to language as well, but in a different manner. It cares about the relation between things and provides contextual understanding of meaning and metaphors that only it can make sense of, making distant connections between words. This metaphoric understanding, which the LH is wholly incapable of and only the RH has an affinity for, is important enough that we will encounter it again and again. It underlies all forms of understanding whatsoever — science, philosophy, poetry, music and other arts alike — and is therefore integral in facilitating our understanding of reality at large.

Importantly, the RH roots everything in context with the experienced world. Only once this integrated, interrelated whole is (partially) given to the LH for analysis does it re-presents the world with use of abstractions, breaking the whole into parts, an act which decontextualises. During this process, the multilayered and implicit meaning gets lost. The multidimensional meaning of a red rose is for the RH to understand only, while the left is skillful only is the use of symbols that are abstract and have a 1-to-1 mapping to an explicit meaning, such as the red traffic light for the command "stop".

This difference is further reflected in the preference of the RH for living and natural things, as opposed to the preference of the LH for the mechanical and artificial. When recognising patterns and classifying them, the left does so in a simple and abstract manner, with broad classes that encompass a variety of objects. Only the RH can identify uniqueness and individuals within a category, such as different varieties of fruits or different models of cars that are simply bundled together by the LH under the label "fruit" or "car".

Our evolving understanding of hemispheric differences revealed that "Because of the way in which the left hemisphere is biased towards identification by parts, and the right hemisphere towards the whole picture, they also differ in the way they understand what they experience.", as Iain puts it. As mentioned above, the principle that attention is prior to things is an important concept in the book. Hopefully, this idea will become clearer while reading this article, but deep reflection is advised. Another principle of utmost importance, is about the fact that there is a hierarchy and circular direction of flow to this attention: 
1. the global awareness of the RH initiates attention;
2. its embodied experience is delivered to the LH for scrutiny and criticism;
3. what has been analysed with the help of the LH is returned to the RH for integration and synthesis.

The last step is crucial, as only the RH is capable of this feat. Without it, we would be lost forever in a hall of mirrors that is the world of the familiar according to the LH.

## Examples in Essential Domains

In essence, there are two modes of being: one in which the RH enables a feeling of connectedness to the whole forever in flux, and one in which the LH enables a stepping back from experience, re-presenting a rather lifeless world that is more useful for manipulation. Both modes are necessary for us to navigate the world, but the latter should serve the former. Iain provides further explanations for the "how" of the hemispheres and their modes of being by illustrating their differences in a few essential domains of the human experience.

**Empathy and Theory of Mind.**\
The characteristics that, arguably, make us most human, are empathy, interconnectedness with others, and our wondrous "theory of mind" ability. These and closely related ones allowed humanity to form unique social networks, where strangers can become partners in life and work in a matter of minutes. The interest of the RH in others, its comprehension of the affective state of others, and its preponderance of emotional understanding makes it the mediator of social behaviour and is largely responsible for the mentioned characteristics, especially when it comes to ascribing mental states to others. Without its contributions, identifying facial expressions, prosody, and gesture becomes arduous to outright impossible. Although the LH does partake when displaying some superficial emotions, it remains overall relatively neutral. There is also no evidential support to split up "positive" and "negative" emotions between the hemispheres, but there is a broad agreement for the RH being more in tune with sadness and less with anger and aggression, where in turn the LH with its dopamine based system activates more. Iain highlights what the RH enables by explaining "The more we are aware of and empathically connected to whatever it is that exists apart from ourselves, the more we are likely to suffer. Sadness and empathy are highly correlated."

**Reason and Rationality.**\
Although the LH is often seen as taking over the whole process of reasoning, both hemispheres use reason, but differ in the way how they do it. Whereas the LH is superior in linear, sequential argument, certain forms of deduction, judgement and mathematical reasoning depend mainly on the RH. In general, evidence shows mathematical reasoning to involve both hemispheres, but the "aha" insight moments, as colloquially expressed, are associated with areas in the RH and is subjectively reported to happen most when not explicitly thinking (in the way of the LH) about the problem. As said before, the LH also hosts the language center itself, and, together with its nature of handling representations, it boasts a complex syntax and large vocabulary when using language. However, both hemispheres partake in language. The RH does so more silently and inhibitory, with damaged areas in the right lobes leading to meaningless hypertrophy of language. The RH plays the vital role in understanding broader meaning of sentences and stories, embracing also non-literal elements like intonation and pragmatics, making interpretation of irony, humor, and sarcasm possible at all. It can deal with implicit, non-verbal communication, helping to detect lies.

**Self-View and Self-Righteousness.**\
Although both hemispheres have (contralateral) motory and sensory connections to the body, only the RH carries a whole-body image, a living image of oneself that is intimately linked to activities of the embodied self in the world, giving the RH also a greater proprioceptive awareness. The RH would say "I **am** the body", whereas the saying of the LH would be "I **have** a body", similar to the distinction in German for the word body as *Leib* (from *leben*, to live) vs. *Körper* (related to corpse, the word having been introduced for medical terminology, with a theological connotation as it would be used after the soul left the body). Its link to the uncertainty to the world of experience makes only the RH able to say "I don't know", whereas the LH needs certainty and needs to be right, evidence showing that the LH confabulates and makes wrong inferences and explanations while always believing to be right, even when shown to be wrong. Only the RH can hold several ambiguous possibilities in suspension without coming to a premature conclusion. Perhaps related is the tendency of the RH to have a "negative feedback" that brings us back when we tend to go too far in one direction, totally opposed to the "positive feedback" of the LH that reinforces itself with things it already knows and does to keep thinking and doing similarly, ad infinitum. Addictions of various sorts has been shown to be associated with RH damage or deficits. Importantly, the RH can inhibit selfish behaviour and allow us to act more morally, as morality can be seen as a form of experience not deducible from anything else, deeply connected to our emotional sensitivity to others. Closely related to selfless actions is altruism. It is a behaviour that astonishes thinkers dominated by the LH, and they have to come up with more intricate and elaborate explanations on how the selfish organism could benefit, just like more and more epicycles has to be added in pre-Keplerian astronomy to save the phenomena. As puzzling as this is to some, the RH truly allows for genuine empathy and altruistic behaviour in humans that is not at all congenial to LH thinking.

**Two Ways of Knowing.**\
When explaining that the RH is more engaged with novel experience, which eventually becomes familiar "information" governed by the LH, we tend to see the brain as an information processing machine. However, a much more nuanced understanding of the brain is one that involves the nature of knowledge itself and the two uses of the word "know". To "know someone" (Latin: *cognoscere*, German: *kennen*) is to have unique knowledge about that person which can't be transferred to someone else, as descriptions are insufficient to convey the whole feelings you have. The knowledge is uniquely relational as it involves you and what different aspects you and your attention brought out in that person, which differs from what someone else may have evoked. The knowledge is thus characterised by "uniqueness", "betweenness", and "whole", which are all aspects of the world according to the RH. To "know facts" (Latin: *sapere*, German: *wissen*) is to have transferable, repeatable, inanimate, and static bits of information, where knowledge is built together from pieces. It is the time and context independent knowledge permitted by science and the type of knowledge created and used by the LH. Carl Jung said that "all cognition is akin to recognition", by which he meant that we come to know in the sense of "cognise" (*wissen*) something only by re-cognising (*erkennen*) something we already knew (*kennen*). The idea that knowledge, perception, and in fact all experience exists only in the relations between things is another one of the recognitions that emerge from the book's thesis.

## Expansion of the Left Hemisphere

The expansion of the anterior region in the RH is tied to the increasingly complex social dynamics of humans during their evolution, which entailed the need to understand others's minds and emotions. But what drove the expansion of the posterior region of the left hemisphere? Although the language center sits within the LH, there are difficulties in believing that the expansion was necessitated by the development of language. Fossil records of the earliest hominids show this anatomical asymmetry long before it is believed that language has developed. Other species, such as great apes, also show this expansion without possessing sophisticated language. 

More likely is the hypothesis, that the deeper drive to grasp, manipulate, and control the world according to the LH drove the expansion. Essentially, the main driver was the accompanying creation of tools, which was ultimately what allowed humans to compete with other species and rise to global dominance. This hypothesis is supported in various ways. Anatomically speaking, the "grasp center" and language center are close together, speaking even activating the other. They are so closely connected that tying hands can impede the fluency of speech and people with no upper limbs claim to not be able to suppress their phantom limbs gesticulating while speaking. Then there is the matter of our vocabulary itself. We "grasp" what someone says and use words that have tactile metaphors embedded within them like impression, expression, or intend (from Latin *tendere*, to reach with the hand), especially prominent in Germanic languages as exemplified by German words like *begreifen*, *erfassen*, *überlegen*, and so on. Phrases that express uncertainty like "I can't put my finger on it" or "I can't get a hold of it" provide further substantiation. It seems that language, gesture, and bodily movement are different actualisations of the same process. Language is an "elaboration, extension, and abstraction of sensorimotor function", according to some researchers, originating in a proto-language formed by the "utterances that were coincident with and driven by the same rhythm as the movement in question". 

When inspecting the workings of one's own mind, it becomes clear that the majority of thought, decisions, and evaluations are done without language. On the contrary, language rather inhibits the fluid nature of thoughts, which are mainly of perceptual and visual nature. Historical anecdotes of prominent scientific figures such as Einstein, Poincaré, and Gauss, amongst others, have ascribed little to no linguistic use to their breakthrough thoughts and thinking patterns. A variety of experiments with other animals also support the claim, that concept formation, forward planning, and theory of mind must predate language in evolutionary terms. Language may have been a sufficient condition for us to become the marvelous thinking and problem-solving entities that we are, but there is no basis to think that it was a necessary condition.

To summarize, our intrinsic need to grasp and manipulate the world, partially fueled by evolutionary pressures, led to our increasing capacity for conceptualisation and symbolic manipulation, which manifested as advancements in tool-making and the communication thereof. Evidence also shows that words make concepts more mentally stable and available to memory, allowing for communication and crafting instructions to be carried over greater temporal and spatial distances. Together, language and tool-making formed a symbiotic relationship that drove, and further benefited from, the expansion of the LH.

## On Metaphors

With the creation of language came the creation of a gap to the experienced world. With language we create a conceptual interface that lives between us and the world. Language is essentially a closed and self-referential network, where the elements (words) are defined and explained in terms of others. Since we can only understand and express things in terms of something else, every word must, eventually, lead us out of this web, as we are otherwise unable to cross the gap to reality again. To do so, the fundamental referential, and crucially, metaphorical layer is our embodied existence, making even words like virtual and immaterial come from the Latin words of *virtus*, a man's strength, and *materia*, the feel of a piece of wood. 

Metaphor, from the Greek words *meta*, across, and *pherein*, carry, serves as the vehicle to cross that separation created by language. Metaphors embody thoughts and place them in a living context, making them therefore intelligible only to the RH. They function because of this last referential layer that brings everything back to the human body and its experience. They also allow for us to sense commonality between two things across the gap, such as "a clash of arguments" (a thing at the level of language) and "a clash of swords" (a thing at the level of experience). Furthermore, metaphors "work both ways", such that saying a man is like a wolf makes the wolf also more manlike. Metaphors are essential in epistemology and without them we literally could not understand the world.

## Attention Shapes Truth

As spoken of above, the fact that attention is ontologically prior to things has profound ramifications. What a thing is depends on who is attending it and in what way. Thus, **attention is a moral act**, for it brings certain aspects into being while making other recede, all depending on our way of approach and what choices we make along the way. It is the reason why we neither discover a totally objective reality "out there" nor invent a completely subjective one. Via a reciprocal evocation, the world "calls forth" something in us while we in turn "call forth" something in the world. Just as music arises from the "betweenness" of the piano and the pianist, so does every aspect of an object emerge from the interplay of us and the world.

This relates to epistemology at large and how we get to know anything, and how we know whether something is true. There are two intertwined reasons why knowing truth is convoluted. First, there is the fact that things in our world vary depending on context, just like in relativity theory the observed properties of an object vary depending on the reference frame under consideration. Second, the nature of the mind (which does the knowing) is inevitably part of this context, and the way in which we attend further shapes that very context. This entanglement of mind and world is at the heart of philosophical and epistemological endeavours to understand truth. It is also interesting to note that the Latin word for truth, *verum*, has a common etymological origin with the Sanskrit word which means to choose or believe, linking to the perennial realisation of man that truth is also an act of where one chooses to place one's trust — an act of faith.

The German philosopher Martin Heidegger saw truth as a process of unconcealments, where acts of attention remove more and more layers, and that this journey of removing things is itself part of the truth. This unconcealing, however, inevitably comes with a concealing of other things, so that there is no ultimate vantage point from where everything can be ultimately seen and known. This claim of such a "view from nowhere" was spoken of before in the context of the scientific enterprise. A related view is that of the philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein, who expressed scepticism of the scientific method because of its attempts to "reduce" things to more fundamental entities that can be known in an ultimate way. It can never be our job to reduce anything to anything, Wittgenstein expresses, for everything is what it is and not another thing. Essentially, the question of how we get to know anything and its entailing scientific investigation always needs a philosophical stance, succinctly put by the philosopher and cognitive scientist Daniel Dennett as "There is no such thing as philosophy-free science; there is only science whose philosophical baggage is taken on board without examination."

## Awareness, Appreciation, and Understanding of the Hemispheres

Conundrums, paradoxes, and dilemmas that arose throughout history, especially in philosophy, can be newly examined and, to a certain degree, resolved by understanding the dynamics between the hemispheres, i.e. what each one does and cannot do. Especially, the failure or inability of the LH to recognise a *Gestalt* and a process is most applicable to many famous "paradoxes" in philosophical thought, such as "The heap of grains of sand of Sorites" or "The ship of Theseus". What they have in common is the view on the world and its processes as an infinite series of static moments, such that there is eternal disagreement between lines of demarcation and phase transitions, all signs of LH predominance. Furthermore, the spectre of epistemological circularity is often raised when trying to understand the whole by its constituents, since they depend on the context as given by the whole. Only the RH mode of being can identify a *Gestalt* or whole entity at any moment, forming understanding holistically and based on the wholeness only it can perceive. The RH does not see paradoxes, but only entities that are "in-formation".

It is instructive to further understand how the appreciation of the hemispheres differs by looking at the hierarchy of values as devised by the German philosopher Max Scheler, which form a pyramid with levels from bottom to top that go as:
- *Sinnliche Werte*, values of utility and pleasure;
- *Lebenswerte*, values that are noble and admirable, such as humility or courage, as well as their counterparts like arrogance and cowardice;
- *Geistige Werte*, values of the spirit and the intellect, which are primarily justice, beauty, and truth;
- *Das Heilige*, essentially values of the holy and sacred.

What is relevant for the thesis of the book, is that the RH sees the lower ones in service of the higher ones, whereas the LH sees exactly the opposite. But what the neurophysiological evidence (and the historical investigation that is the topic of the second part of the book) shows, is that the modes of operation of the LH are applicable only, and then merely in a limited fashion, to the lowermost level of the pyramid. The higher levels require empathetic, affective, and moral engagement with the world as enabled by the RH.

According to the LH, we are a passive computer or camera, such that what we see "out there" is imprinted on us via a manner equivalent to passive data collection. It is a cold and detached stance that is, unfortunately one may add, the basis of the scientific and bureaucratic mind. Taken to its ultimate conclusions, it leads to a place where we don't wish to follow: the dead end that is nihilism. The relationship that the LH brings via the scientific method and its implied materialism, is not a non-existence of a relationship of us and the world, but rather a disengaged one that implies, incorrectly, observers having no impact on the observed, as well as there being no altering effect on the observing person caused by what the person observes and how. The "betweenness" we encountered before is not absent, just denied.

Whatever denial is employed, the fact remains that how we attend to the world changes not just other things and people, but also who we are. We must be conscious of what we spend our time attending to — and how.

## "False Friends" of the Hemispheres

Two words in different languages that may look or sound similar but carry a completely different meaning are often referred to as "false friends". There are a few common words, whose meaning and interpretation differs substantially depending on which hemisphere explains them. Some of the more illustrative ones covered in the book are the following.

**Belief.**\
For the LH, belief is laced with uncertainty, doubt, or outright falsity, making it a lesser form of the certain knowing and thinking that defines its mode of operation. For the RH, belief is a matter of caring and having a relationship with someone or something. For example, the statements "I believe in you" or "I believe in God" are not, as espoused by the RH, implying that one thinks or knows something with a degree of uncertainty, but rather reflect having an attitude towards something, a certain disposition towards an aspect of the world. With it come characteristic behaviours and responsibilities.

**Will.**\
Willing shares properties with believing, in that it is connected to having a certain disposition towards the world. For the LH it is one of use and control, but for the RH, with its open and caring disposition that values relationships, will relates to a desire or longing towards something, towards "Other".

**Familiarity.**\
The LH sees generic and general things to be familiar, those things that have constantly been re-presented by it and have thus been ontologically demoted and emptied of meaning. Essentially the opposite is the case for the RH, which sees those parts of the world as familiar that are constantly present, but have thus been enriched over time with meaning.

**Newness.**\
Related to the above, newness, for the RH, is perhaps best described as a seeing for the first time what seems only too familiar through a patient openness that cannot be willed. Contrarily, the sense of novelty for the LH is constructed at will via the recombination of already known elements, such that the resulting distance from the familiar, uniqueness, and bizarreness shocks us into novelty.

**Activity & Passivity.**\
Loss of control and loss of self-determination is seen as passivity from the perspective of the LH. Consequently, only a restoration thereof means that you are active. But it is exactly this passivity that the RH endorses, this patient openness as required for newness, enabling things to come to you less by what is done and more by what is not done. The RH sees activity in the not doing part, which makes it able to experience both activity and passivity in the same process or experience, a feat that is denied to the LH with its "either/or" thinking.

## The Primacy of the Right Hemisphere

Neurophysiological evidence clearly indicated the RH to have precedence over the LH. The RH underwrites all knowledge the LH comes to have, as it alone is the first point of contact with experience and decides what information gets passed along. The Primacy of the RH is further evidenced by the fact that it alone is capable of synthesising the fragments of knowledge from both hemispheres into a usable whole.

Besides visible in scientific evidence, the order of exchange between the hemispheres is mirrored in many other processes as well, in various domains and on various scales. Foreshadowing the contents and analysis of the second part of the book, Iain explains that we can see a similarity of the cycle of philosophy to the way information is cycled through the hemispheres: What started as a sense of awe, wonder, and curiosity as lived through the RH progressed through inspections from a dizzying amount of angles using the mechanistic logic and language of the LH, which were gradually synthesised because of the realisation that language, linearity, and certainty must themselves be transcended with the help of the RH, ending in the painstakingly regained profound sense of wonder of the 20th century. 

This and other similar analyses have a common theme underlying them all, again reflecting the primacy of the RH: Metaphor, as subserved by the RH, comes before denotation, as subserved by the LH. It is a historical truth, as well as an epistemological fact, as one cannot draw something away and make it abstract (Latin: *abs-*, away, and *trahere*, pull) unless there is already something to draw it away from. In other words, one cannot unfold something and make it explicit (Latin: *ex-*, out, and *plicare*, fold) unless it is already folded. We experience many things being implicit in their nature. Making them explicit, as we do via language, changes their nature. Too much of this explicitness, too much self-awareness, and we destroy meaning until things become mechanical and lifeless.

Only the frame of mind of the RH makes certain things possible, such as going to sleep, entering a flow state, or cultivating and exhibiting wisdom. Importantly, the RH allows for a "semi-opaque" view, where we rest our attention on the object while retaining awareness of the depth given by its contextual and environmental embedding. The spotlight vision of the LH effectively projects things onto a plane and transforms it into abstract shapes in the process. It does so in an attempt to see something "clearly", such that we can exercise control. However, it is precisely this partial transparency that makes us see a painting neither as an amalgam of colored patches nor as a mere window, but as a layer *through* which we can optimally grasp reality, while being part of it.

## Before Cognition

An inevitable component to include in discussions about awareness, decisions, and our conscious processing thereof is the work of Benjamin Libet in 1985 (and earlier work by Hans Kornhuber). The work showed that the urge to follow through with the action of one’s choosing is only seen in the neurophysiological data about 0.2 seconds after the so called "readiness potential", which roughly measures motor cortex activity that leads to muscle movement. In a way, the mind knew that its owner was going to decide to act before carrying out the action. Naturally, the findings spawned a myriad of intellectual debates about free will. It is mentioned in the book partly because Iain expresses his opinion, that this work only becomes an issue when believing solely the conscious parts of the mind to constitute the "me", a notion he does not endorse. In a world after the works of Freud, Jung, and others about the vastness of our subconscious, it is even perplexing that such limiting views of the self are still held by many.

Another landmark study, which is more closely related to the two worlds of the hemispheres, is that of David McNeill on gestures and speech. It revealed that thought originates in the RH with a "largely imagistic and minimally analytic" nature, focusing on the *Gestalt* and holistic description of the thought content, which is gesticular in nature. Only afterwards does the LH get involved by providing a linguistic structure with which to integrate these thoughts, and by the moment of utterance there is a synthesis of both holistic and analytic cognitive functions as provided by the RH and LH, respectively. A particularly striking finding concerns the situation where and when there is a mismatch between gesture and speech: it is **always** the gesture that carries the meaning of the thought correctly to its conclusions when there is a verbal mistake. Iain concludes that "The evidence from McNeill's work is that — temporally, logically and ontologically — the right-hemisphere world grounds that of the left."

That the implicit grounds the explicit is also supported by research that shows us to first make an intuitive assessment of the whole before any cognitive processes come into play, though they will be used later to explain and justify our choices. That such emotional reactions and feelings precede full cognitive processing — affect coming first, thinking later — is referred to as the **primacy of affect**. It is made sense of by further evidence suggesting that perceptual-cognitive awareness appears to have developed on the back of (or, anatomically speaking, on top of) affective awareness that served as a "revolutionary prerequisite" in the words of the neuroscientist Jaak Panksepp, and he further writes that the sentiment of Descartes "I think, therefore I am" may be actually superseded by "I feel, therefore I am". Emotion and the body are the irreducible core of experience, reason emanating from it to give us the ability to limit and direct emotions, so much so that the French philosopher Vauvenargues has said "emotion has taught mankind to reason". Even the prejudice we have in favour of reason cannot itself be justified by reasoning — we intuit the virtues of reason.

These studies and others enriched our understanding in the temporal aspects of our awareness and cognition, and showed that the processes and contents of the RH and LH are not, in a way, synchronized with each other. Interestingly, the resulting small windows during the various communications between areas in the brain allows the hemispheres to either not permit or permit aspects of being to be presented (re-presented for the LH) by saying no or not saying no, respectively. The hemispheres are apophatic and inhibitory in nature, which is reflected in the functional anatomy of the brain, specifically in the most highly evolved part of the brain, the frontal cortex. It achieves what it does by (not) negating other brain activity. Another piece to address potential restrictions about free will as raised by Libet's experiments is the fact that there is time between unconscious initiation and conscious execution, such that we have time to intervene and "veto" the action. In this sense we may possess "free won't" rather than free will.

## Limitations of the System

As explained above, it is vital that the divided and broken down world of the LH must eventually be returned to the unifying worldview of the RH, just as Hegel's thesis and anti-thesis are supposed to be brought together to form a "syn-thesis" at a higher level. This necessity is connected to the theorems and their implications of the mathematician Kurt Gödel, when one recognises that the models created and employed by the LH resemble axiomatic systems. Loosely speaking, Gödel showed that there will always be statements that can arise from within a system whose truth the system itself cannot ascertain. What this means for the LH is that its rational means of pursuit can be impotent when it comes to achieving some of its own rationally conceived goals. It exposes the limits of the system and points beyond, in this case to the RH. It is perhaps akin to another one of Hegel's terms, namely *Aufhebung* ("sublation" in English). It denotes a way in which later stages of an "organic" process are lifted up and transformed while imbuing meaning to earlier stages that may have even been incompatible or inconsistent with one another. In Hegel's words then, the results of the LH are *aufgehoben* by the RH, such that they are not completely dismissed, but not taken literally either.

The systems that are created and seen as complete by the LH are, not just by the standards of the RH, but even in its own terms, incomplete. "Whether or not the superstructure holds up," remarks Iain, "their [the systems'] foundations lie in, and are ‘bootstrapped’ on, intuition: the premises from which the rational system building begins, and even the rational mode of operation itself, that of the value of reason, cannot be confirmed by the process of rationalistic systematisation, but need ultimately to be intuited." This perfectly captures the dilemma of the LH, which ultimately forces it to pass the scepter back to the RH. 

## The Relationship of the Hemispheres at Three Levels

The last overarching topic of the first part of the book, and therefore for the remainder of this article, is that of the relationship the hemispheres have with one another. Their interaction is analysed on three different levels: the neurological, the phenomenological, and the last being perhaps best put as the axiological level, which consists of a number of asymmetries of different kinds.

### Level One

On the automatic, moment-to-moment, responsive level, on which decisions are made in milliseconds, the hemispheres are rather in opposition than collaboration. Here, once a hemisphere assumes control of a certain task — a decision based on a set of expectations concerning the nature of the task — it remains in control even if the other hemisphere would be better suited to the task and give better performance. This step happens before any actual rigorous information processing, and likely evolved as an inferior but quick solution, which is better than an inefficient back and forth information transfer across the hemispheres that delays a pressing, or perhaps even vital answer. The whole process is inhibitory in nature.

### Level Two

On the phenomenological level, where hemispheric interactions bring our world of experience into being, there are no neurological correlates to be measured, but rather deductions are made from what we know of the nature and typical modes of operations of each hemisphere. Since it is in the best interest of both hemispheres to deliver an interpretation of the world that promotes survival, at this higher level cooperation is required. Through repeatable results and reproducible data in experiments and studies where the hemispheres are isolated, the active regions within each hemisphere for different types of tasks have become predictable by neuroscience. However, and this was a major finding, this is not true when both hemispheres work together. The activity patterns, as well as the nature of processing cannot be predicted from what we know of the parts, as there are emergent properties, patterns, and functions. Another intriguing observation that arises at this level is that of "hemispheric utilisation bias", which makes one hemisphere predominate, either overall or for particular kinds of experience, the bias being stronger if the LH has predominance. Despite all this, we seem to experience a single unified field of consciousness. Why this is so is a fascinating question that remains open until this day.

### Level Three

The last level is characterised by two asymmetries that indicate where the power ought to lie, namely with the RH, as well as three asymmetries that, however, skew the power balance in favour of the LH.

First, there is the **ontological asymmetry**, which exists due to the RH being the mediator of experience from which the world of the LH is derived from, and therefore depends on. Second, there is an **asymmetry of function**, where the products of the LH must be returned to the RH in order to make sense and to live on within something new. Although the transfer of content from the RH to the unpacking, dismantling, and analysing processes of the LH can enrich experience and further our understanding, it is not literally vital as the reverse process is. Both these asymmetries together undeniably indicate that the RH ought to be the Master and the LH his Emissary.

What makes the worldview of the LH more accessible is its proximity to our self-awareness, self-inspecting intellect. The views of the LH are more readily defensible and expressible via language and logic that are mostly controlled by it. The LH thus benefits more from an inherent **asymmetry of means**. Over time, the systems built by the LH also give solidity and permanence to its worldview, enabling easy transfer of knowledge that the RH with its complex, ever-changing, and holistic information cannot do. The self-referential and closed nature of these systems reflect an **asymmetry of structure**, treating anything not expressible via language or logic as irrelevant. Finally, the transfer process between the hemispheres favors the LH in an **asymmetry of interaction**. The unfolding and analysis that the LH performs to build its systems can get dragged out and further substantiated internally via the systems already built, effectively creating a positive feedback loop. As one will see in part two of the book, this substantiation of the last asymmetry can even happen externally in the corporeal world that we have learned to control and manipulate to an enormous degree.

Whether one adopts a metaphysics more towards the side of materialism or one in the direction of idealism (or any other), it seems unavoidable to address the questions raised when studying the brain — the vehicle, facilitator, conjurer, or conduit of thought, consciousness, and our existence.
