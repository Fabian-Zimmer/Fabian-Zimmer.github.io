The Master and His Emissary by Iain McGilchrist

  • neuroscience
  • hypothesis
  • worldview

This article is about part 1 of the book The Master and His Emissary by Iain McGilchrist. In part 1, Iain gives us a comprehensive overview of the anatomy of our brain, what cognitive functions and abilities are associated with which hemisphere, and how the hemispheres each see and interact with the self, the world and subjective experience. Due to their frequent use, right hemisphere is abbreviated as RH and left hemisphere as LH.

Causes for lateralisation

Brain lateralisation is a widespread phenomenon in the animal kingdom. The left/right asymmetry in the central nervous system is especially strong across all vertebrate classes (amphibians, birds, fish, mammals, and reptiles). Other animal groups (e.g. arthropods or mollusks) show species-specific lateralisation (e.g. bees & ants or octopodes). Motor control in animals with brain lateralisation is contralateral, i.e. the LH control the right side of the body, and the RH the left side. It is also not a recent evolutionary trait, as bi-hemispheric structure goes back a long way down the phylogenic tree.

This seems to suggest, that there was a universal problem that biological organisms faced, and evolution brought forth an efficient solution that spread through almost all ecological niches. What common problem do all living things share, that are pressured by selective forces? The answer seems to lie with attention. For successful proliferation, an organism must have the capacity to do two things simultaneously:

  1. having a broad and open awareness of the environment and terrain, especially scanning for threats, like predators or natural hazards;
  2. having a narrow and focused attention to pick out important things from this background environment, like food or potential mates.

These two modes require two very different kinds of attention. They reflect the conflict between individual needs, being in competition with others, and seeing oneself in context and part of a larger whole, in relation to others.

Another motivation why a division of the brain seems beneficial is based on an observation by Charles Sherrington (1906): human sensorimotor control relies on “opponent processors”, i.e. dual forces that oppose and inhibit each other, allowing us to perform delicate procedures with our appendages. This argument could, however, be considered close to begging the question.

Whatever the exact set of causes and conditions were that led to divided brains, animals who are lateralised show better capacity and ability in important survivalistic areas, like hunting, foraging, and socializing.

Anatomical Overview

Our divided brain is connected by the Corpus Callosum, a tissue containing millions of fibres that connect topologically similar regions of each hemisphere. Out of the $\mathcal{O}(80)$ billion neurons, on the $\mathcal{O}(20)$ billion are cortical neurons.1 But only about 2% of those cortical neurons are connected via this tissue, and such connections are often inhibitory, suppressing the communication across hemispheres.2 Remarkably, the Callosum has decreased in size with increasing overall brain size, becoming smaller during our evolutionary history. While the functional asymmetry between hemispheres grew, the interconnectedness lessened. We can conclude tentatively, that each hemisphere was increasingly less able to interfere with the other.

There are many differences between the hemispheres, both of anatomical and functional nature. To get a first impression of each hemisphere, the following columns capture some important aspects of each. All mentioned items and concepts will be frequently revisited, each time deepening our understanding.

Right Hemisphere

The RH is longer, wider, and heavier. It has more and larger neurons, more dendritic branching,[^3] and more white matter,[^4] all arranged to facilitate global communication across the whole hemisphere. The RH is more reliant on nor-adrenaline, which enables sustained performance over longer periods. Together with the fact, that the RH is able to take in multiple types of sensory data together, it is reasonably analogous to a parallel processor. All of this is together provides a global, broad, and flexible attention that allows the RH to present the world — it sees things as a whole and embedded in context, which you are an integral part of.

The RH presents the world as it is, and as a general rule, no models or predictions are involved. It is the part of our brain that can provide novelty to us. This is supported by studies showing metabolic activity in the RH to be increased when practicing new skills, or when new information enters peripheral vision.

And finally, the frontal lobe of the RH is asymmetrically larger than the same region in the LH. Metabolism in this part is linked to activities concerning empathy, socialising, and in general the relationships between ourselves and individuals.

Left Hemisphere

The LH shows many contrasting and opposite characteristics compared to the RH. Importantly, it is wired in a local fashion: regions within the LH are interconnected within themselves, with only relatively sparse connections across regions and larger distances. The resulting characteristic of the LH is its narrow-minded and self-referential nature.

The LH hosts the language center, which is associated with its enlarged posterior region. Instead of nor-adrenaline, it relies more on dopamine. These two properties already characterise the LH well, and lay the foundations to the process most associated with the LH: logic and language. For linguistic phrases to be constructed, a chain of logical events that are ordered in a mostly serial and linear fashion is required. The motivation to complete these chains is provided by the euphoric sensations delivered by the dopamine micro doses. As such, the LH is reasonably analogous to a serial processor.

The LH receives what is present from the RH. It then re-presents the world in de-contextualised abstractions, the whole broken into individual parts to be logically analysed with local and focused attention. Once category systems for stimuli and past events, as well as predictive models for the future have been built, the LH has changed novelty to familiarity, surprise to routine, presentation to re-presentation.

The story above applies to the “standard” configuration of the brain shared by about 90% of people: the language center sits in the LH and the right-hand is dominant. The the other 10% can have partial of complete hemispheric inversion, where some or all processes and centers usually associated with the LH are associated with the RH, and vice versa. Not much may happen in the latter (complete) case, but unique benefits or deficits are documented for the former (partial) case.

Generally, increased size comes with increased functional capacity. According to the overview above, the RH is overall more capable then. It will become clear later, that the RH is not just more powerful, but also is supposed to hold primacy… a status that has been usurped by the LH in the modern world.

Philosophical Interlude

There are a few musings we can make at this point, before moving on to more tangible matters.

The most fascinating unknown related to the brain is that of mind and consciousness. There is nothing remotely like it for means of comparison. It is phenomenologically and ontologically unique. That makes investigations difficult, since “All attempts at explanation depend, whether explicitly or implicitly, on drawing parallels between the thing to be explained and some other thing that we believe we already understand better.” as Iain puts it. The closest analogy he gives in a footnote is based on the metaphor of wave and water. Does the water movement cause the wave? Not really, for the flow of water is the wave. Analogously, the changing states of the brain do not cause the mind, but are the mind. But that’s where the analogy ends, since waves do not have this “inwardness” that the mind does through its self-reflection abilities.

Another matter encountered when studying the brain and mind, is that everything we know about them is itself a product of consciousness. The difficulty in the quest of determining whether mind depends on matter and/or is a product of the brain, is that everything we know about anything is itself a mental creation. What Iain emphasises at this point, is that the mind is more a process than a thing, a way of being rather than an entity. A process of interaction with whatever it is that exists apart from ourselves according to its own private history.

Finally, and of utmost importance for the central thesis of the book, is the following tenet. Ontologically speaking, attention is prior to functions and things, since the world (in which functions and things exist) is altered depending on the way we attend it. Attention changes what kind of things come into being for us. In other words, while “cognitive functions” may be neutral, the way they are used via attention is how values enter, and how our perception of reality is being shaped. For example, two participants of a social interaction would have very different feelings and views when they treat each other as friends or enemies, even though nothing objectively has changed. Science purports to not do this, claiming to instead objectively uncover the truth in everything with a “view from nowhere”. However this stance is also value-laden in itself: valuing detachment of the observer to the observed thing. Useful, but not truer or more real than other viewpoints.
